It is common practice in biology to "genetically engineer" organisms. In bacteria, it is actually surprisingly simple: you put some salt on them, add some DNA to their water, heat them up, cool them down, and then feed them tasty broth to recover from their sauna and cold plunge. It's been done this way since the 70s. The process is pretty forgiving: the length of time that you heat or cool them is somewhat flexible and you only need one bacterium of thousands to take up the DNA to be successful.

I figured it would be a good exercise in scientific communication to explain this method, called heat shock transformation, and illustrate the mechanism. But despite how widespread and common it is, it turns out that we don't actually understand exactly what is happening with the membranes and molecules.

The salt, we get. The specific salt is calcium chloride, which is primarily for the calcium. DNA and the bacterial membrane are both negatively charged, so normally they would repel each other. Calcium is a positively charged ion, and allows the DNA to get close to the bacteria.

I got my competent cells pre-mixed with the calcium chloride, shipped frozen. Since you only really need one, you can work with tiny quantities, like a drop of bacteria (containing millions), and a fifth of that of DNA. The specific quantities also aren't crazy important, which is also something I found surprising in science, where one imagines everything requiring precision to work.

The whole process of heat shock transformation:

1. Mix your smidge of DNA in with a drop of competent cells.
2. Keep on ice for 5–30 minutes.
3. Heat at ~42°C for 30–60 seconds.
4. Put back on ice for 1–2 minutes.
5. Add ~1/8 tsp nutrient rich media.
6. Incubate at 37°C for 20–60 minutes.

That's it. Cold → hot → cold, and somehow, some fraction of your cells end up with your DNA inside them.

Somehow. We still somehow don't know how this works. There are some hypotheses involving membrane fluidity and pressure. We know the recipe. We're still not sure what's actually happening biologically.

There was something oddly reassuring about learning that, honestly. I'd spent so long treating this protocol as a black box that I hadn't been diligent enough to investigate, and it turns out the box is black for everyone. I think it's impressive that something that we don't understand can survive in scientific practice for so long, and it definitely changed my view of how science works. This is an empirical practice, trial and error, not just theory.
